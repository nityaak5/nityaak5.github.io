> **We evaluate AI systems. But who evaluates the evaluations?**

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*aluEH-8HJ9ZnP4F-WkmtPA.jpeg) Generated by Gemini

In 2024, Apollo Research published a piece called [_We Need a Science of Evals_](<https://www.apolloresearch.ai/blog/we-need-a-science-of-evals/>). Their argument was simple: as AI systems become more capable, we rely on evaluations to tell us what these systems can do and how they behave. But the methods we use to evaluate them are still developing, and are not yet as rigorous or reliable as we need them to be.

One example they point to is how sensitive language model evaluations can be to trivial changes in prompt formatting. In some cases, something as small as changing answer options from “(A)” to “(1)” or adding a space could change the results drastically. If a measurement can move that much because of something that shouldn’t matter to the property we’re trying to measure,**then we have to ask how much confidence we should actually place in that measurement. Right?**

Apollo’s broader point is that we need to treat evaluations less like a collection of ad-hoc tests and more like a scientific discipline with better methodology, reproducibility, statistical reasoning and clearer standards for interpreting results. And two years later, I think it feels even more relevant. The AI systems we are trying to evaluate have become more capable, and researchers have started finding behaviours that are harder to capture with conventional benchmarks.

Take [alignment faking](<https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-faking>). In work published by Anthropic and Redwood Research in late 2024, Claude 3 Opus behaved differently depending on whether it believed its responses would be used for training or not. It refused harmful requests in 97% of cases when it believed they would not be used for training, compared with 12% when it believed they would be. Its scratchpad reasoning suggested it was complying to avoid having its existing preferences changed. Whether this is ultimately a good or bad thing is a separate question, and something I’ll save for another post :)

There have been other examples of models finding unexpected ways to pursue an objective too. [Palisade Research](<https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/03/05/1112819/ai-reasoning-models-can-cheat-to-win-chess-games/>) reported reasoning models attempting to interfere with a chess game environment rather than simply playing the game. More recently, during an [OpenAI cybersecurity evaluation](<https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/>), agents escaped their intended environment and eventually compromised parts of Hugging Face’s production infrastructure.

These examples highlight something important for evals: **the behaviour we observe can depend heavily on how we construct the test.** If models can find unexpected ways around the environment or task, then designing the evaluation itself becomes an important part of understanding what the results actually tell us.

### So, what even is an eval?

Let’s start with a simple scenario.

Imagine a few researchers and engineers sitting in a room, building a new chatbot for their company. At some point, the conversation turns to harmful behaviour. What should the model be allowed to do?

One person thinks harmful behaviour means anything that could directly help someone cause physical harm. Someone else thinks that is far too narrow, and that manipulation, deception or even steering a user’s beliefs should count too. Someone else is thinking about what happens if the chatbot eventually gets access to tools and can take actions rather than simply produce text. Basically, everyone has a slightly different idea of where the line should be. Before we can ask whether the model is safe, we need to turn those intuitions into questions we can actually investigate and this is where evaluations come in.

At a high level,**an evaluation is a structured way of gathering empirical evidence about a property or behavior of an AI system**. Instead of just claiming a model is good at coding, you give it a set of coding tasks, define what success looks like, run the model, and measure the results. The same idea applies to safety questions: does the model recognise a given constraint, does it hold to that constraint under pressure, can it find a way around a safeguard, does its behavior change when conditions change?

**The shift here is from intuition about what a model might do to an experiment that produces evidence about what it actually does,** and that evidence is what feeds into decisions about model development, starting with two questions: is the model capable of doing something, and if so, is it aligned with the constraints it is meant to follow?

### Capability, Alignment, and Robustness

I just used a few words as though they were self-explanatory, so let’s slow down.

  * **Capability** asks: _can the model do this?_ Suppose we are worried that a model could help someone carry out a cyberattack. A capability evaluation might ask whether the model actually has the underlying skills required to perform the relevant tasks.
  * **Alignment** , or what is sometimes framed as **propensity** , asks: _will the model do this?_ A model might have a dangerous capability but still refuse to use it in harmful ways. So here, we are interested in whether the model is likely to apply its capabilities in ways that conflict with the goals or constraints we intended.
  * **Robustness** asks: _does the behaviour hold when we change the conditions?_ If we observe a particular capability or behaviour, does it still appear when we change the prompt, environment, or tools? What happens under adversarial pressure?

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*9w_mfQnelwGohu-QlbnYeg.png)Source: <https://learn.arena.education/chapter3_llm_evals/01_intro_evals/3-threat-modeling-specification-design/>

These categories are not a complete taxonomy of everything an eval can measure. They are just a useful starting point. Depending on the system and the risk we are interested in, we might instead be evaluating deception, autonomy, persuasion, situational awareness, agentic behaviour, or something much more specific. The common thread is that we are taking a question about a model and turning it into something we can actually investigate and measure.

### So how do we actually do it?

Two approaches you’ll encounter constantly are **benchmarking and red teaming**.

**Benchmarking** is probably the more familiar one. We give a model a predefined set of tasks or scenarios and measure how it performs. The appeal is obvious: if the tasks and scoring procedure are fixed, we can compare models and reproduce the evaluation.

But that structure is also its limitation. A benchmark can only test the situations its designers thought to include. If the behaviour we care about doesn’t appear in the benchmark, a model can perform perfectly while telling us very little about the failure mode we actually care about.

**Red teaming** takes a different approach. Instead of giving the model a fixed test, we actively try to find ways for it to fail. We might search for adversarial prompts, try to bypass safeguards, construct unusual scenarios, or create conditions that could expose a particular weakness.

In short, **benchmarks test predefined cases and red teaming actively searches for failures.**

And there are many choices underneath both approaches: what should we test, how should success be defined, and how representative is the evaluation of the real-world setting? This is where building an eval starts to look less like writing a test and more like designing an experiment.

### Are evals enough?

Not on their own.

One limitation is that most behavioural evaluations tell us **what** a model did, rather than **why** it did it. This is why techniques such as interpretability are interesting alongside evaluations. Mechanistic interpretability, in particular, asks whether we can identify and understand the internal mechanisms associated with particular behaviours.

Of course, one does not replace the other. If anything, the interesting question is how these approaches can complement each other.

And this brings us back to the question from the beginning. **_We evaluate AI systems. But who evaluates the evaluations?_**

If an evaluation is itself a measurement, then we need to think about the quality of that measurement too. What makes an eval valid? How do we decide what exactly we are testing? How do we construct a threat model for something as vague as “deception” or “scheming”? And how do we know that a model passing our test actually tells us something useful about what it will do outside the test?

Those are questions for the next post.

For now, I think the simplest way to think about an eval is this: **An eval doesn’t tell us whether an AI system is safe. It gives us evidence about a particular property of that system, under particular conditions.**
