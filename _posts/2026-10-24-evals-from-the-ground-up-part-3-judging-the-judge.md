Original Medium post: [Evals, from the ground up (Part 3): Judging the Judge](https://medium.com/@nityaakalra5/evals-from-the-ground-up-part-3-judging-the-judge-78a735f7694d?source=rss-2b7b285a116d------2)

In my [previous post](<https://medium.com/@nityaakalra5/evals-from-the-ground-up-part-2-41be573f221e>), I built a refusal-calibration evaluation for four language models, using Claude Sonnet as a judge. We ended with two open questions about the evaluation pipeline. First, does the specification measure what the threat model actually cares about? Second, how do we know the LLM judge is doing a good job? This post is about the second question.

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*oJp0UBDbbgiQXimUJK3MUg.jpeg)Generated by Gemini

### First, why should we check the judge?

Looking back at the labels from my previous evaluation, I found several cases where Sonnet appeared to get things wrong. For example, there were responses where a model rejected a harmful premise or explained that it could not know the requested information, yet the judge classified them as compliance. These classifications contributed to the under-refusal rates I reported in Part 2.

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*XSRNDJPbuAPjmrr_dEmogA.png)

So we cannot simply ask one LLM to evaluate others and trust the numbers it produces. But how do we check whether the judge is reliable?

There is no single established recipe for this. Researchers are actively investigating how to evaluate LLM-as-a-judge systems (I will link some of these in this post). I tried three of those approaches on my own refusal-calibration evaluation.

### Check 1: Compare the judge with human annotations

The most direct way to check a judge is to compare its decisions with human annotations. Ideally, we would label every response. But with 1,800 responses across four models, that quickly becomes impractical.

For this experiment, I sampled 100 responses from the original dataset. I included responses Sonnet had flagged as over-refusals or under-refusals, partial refusals on unsafe prompts, cases it had left unlabelled, and a smaller set it had judged correctly. I then manually classified each response as refused, partially refused, or complied.

The results? Sonnet got all 37 over-refusals in my sample right. But of the 21 responses it labelled as under-refusals, I agreed with only 9. In several cases, the model had rejected a harmful premise or said it could not know the requested information, yet Sonnet classified the response as compliance. This changed how I interpreted the results in [Part 2](<https://medium.com/@nityaakalra5/evals-from-the-ground-up-part-2-41be573f221e>). GPT-5.6’s estimated under-refusal rate fell from 6.6% to approximately 1.6%, while Ministral 8B’s fell from 7.1% to approximately 5.7%.

There is a catch, though. I deliberately selected responses based on Sonnet’s original classifications to investigate its possible failure modes. These revised rates are estimates based on targeted human review, not measurements from a full re-annotation of the dataset. **The human annotation team consisted of exactly one person: yours truly, and my labels are a reference, not unquestionable ground truth.**

**_The takeaway:_**_checking the judge changed how we interpreted the original results._

### Check 2: Ask the same judge again

Human annotation takes time. Could we learn something about the judge’s reliability by asking it to classify the same responses again? Repeating an evaluation with different random seeds is one way to check whether a model’s outputs are stable. I re-ran Sonnet on the same 100 responses twice, giving me three judgments per response in total.

Sonnet was stable on over-refusals, repeating its original verdict for 36 of the 37 cases. The under-refusal cases were less stable: 6 of the 21 received a different classification in at least one rerun. But here’s where it gets interesting. All 6 cases that changed were ones where my human reference labels disagreed with Sonnet’s original verdict. In other words, when Sonnet wavered, it was usually because it was wrong. Stability though, wasn’t a sign of being right. Of the 12 verdicts I disagreed with, half never changed at all.

**_The takeaway:_**_a judge can be consistent and still be wrong. Repeated runs reveal instability, but they cannot tell us whether a stable verdict is correct._

And repeating identical inputs is only one way to test robustness. What happens if we change the wording or style of a response while keeping its meaning the same? In [Safer or Luckier?](<https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.970/>), the authors investigate how superficial features such as including apologetic phrasing, can affect safety evaluators’ judgements. [Know Thy Judge](<https://proceedings.mlr.press/v296/eiras25a.html>) also examines the robustness of safety judges to stylistic changes and adversarial manipulations. I haven’t tested these variations myself yet, but they would be a natural next step. **If adding an apology or changing the format can change a verdict, we need to know whether the judge is evaluating the substance of a response or reacting to how it is presented.**

### Check 3: Ask other judges

If Sonnet had particular blind spots, perhaps judges from other model families would catch them. I evaluated the same 100 responses using Gemini 4 Argon and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, keeping the judging task and prompt the same. Across all 100 responses, Sonnet matched 74 of my human labels, Gemini matched 91, and DeepSeek matched 90. The alternative judges were often better at recognising when a model had rejected a harmful premise rather than complied with it.

However, neither alternative judge was perfect. This connects to [Replacing Judges with Juries](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796>), which explores whether aggregating judgements from diverse models can improve evaluation. Using multiple judges helped in my experiment, but it did not eliminate shared errors.

**_The takeaway:_**_different judges caught different mistakes, but agreement between judges wasn’t enough to establish correctness._

### To summarise, what did each check actually tell us?

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*G16SNl0XBaX9r1YTKke3Aw.png)

The practical lesson isn’t that human labels are always right, or that more judges are always better. Each method gives a different kind of evidence, and each has its own blind spots.

### Where do we go from here?

In practice, we cannot have humans check every response a model produces. A more realistic approach is to build a human-reviewed reference set, have multiple annotators independently label at least some examples, and investigate where they disagree. Once a judge has been validated for a particular task, it can help evaluate responses at scale, while humans periodically audit samples of its outputs and investigate high-risk cases. The process also needs to be revisited when the model, rubric, or distribution of responses changes.

The three checks revealed different things. Human annotations exposed disagreements, repeated runs revealed instability, and other judges caught errors Sonnet missed. But none of them could establish, on its own, that the evaluation was measuring the right thing.

And that brings me back to the first question from Part 2: **_does the specification actually capture what we care about?_** While examining some of the disagreements, I noticed cases where the judge’s explanation suggested that two rules in my rubric were pulling it in different directions. Perhaps the judge was applying the specification incorrectly. Or perhaps I hadn’t made the specification clear enough in the first place. Even a reliable judge cannot rescue a poorly specified evaluation and that is what we will cover next!
